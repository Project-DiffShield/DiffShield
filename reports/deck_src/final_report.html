1Abstract

Diffusion-based image editors can turn any face photo posted online into a convincing fake of that person. DiffShield is a defensive tool that the owner of a photo runs before posting it: it adds an almost invisible pattern of noise (no pixel changes by more than 4 of 255 brightness levels) chosen so that edits made from the photo no longer preserve the person’s identity. The noise is found by an optimiser that, at every step, simulates a cleanup an attacker might apply (JPEG compression, resizing, a VAE round trip, or a DiffPure-style diffusion purifier) and then pulls the editor’s internal representation of the cleaned photo toward a fixed, useless target, while also disturbing the editor’s denoiser and penalising any visible change. Its weights were chosen by a systematic search on separately selected tuning photos. An independent evaluation harness attacks the protected photos with standard cleanups and editors, including ones the optimiser never saw, and measures identity with the ArcFace face recogniser. On 100 CelebA-HQ test photos, every protected photo stays invisible (PSNR ≥ 38 dB, SSIM ≥ 0.95), and 51.2% of Stable Diffusion edits no longer match the person, against 13.9% for unprotected photos (+37.3 points, 95% CI [+32.7, +42.2]) and 24.0% for our first prototype (+27.2 [+23.0, +31.7]). The protection survives SD-based DiffPure-style cleanup and the VAE round trip and is ahead of PhotoGuard under JPEG, but it does not extend to SDXL, inpainting or IP-Adapter FaceID, and a pixel-space diffusion purifier removes it.

2Problem Definition, Research Motivation & Literature Review

2.1Problem Statement & Research Motivation

  • Problem Statement: Given a face photo, find a small perturbation (at most 4/255 per pixel) such that (i) the protected photo looks identical to the original, and (ii) when someone edits the protected photo with a diffusion editor, possibly after cleaning it, the edit is no longer recognised as the same person. Scope: an attacker who does not know the protection and uses Stable Diffusion 1.5 or SDXL image-to-image, InstructPix2Pix, SD inpainting or IP-Adapter FaceID, optionally after a cleanup (JPEG, resizing, VAE round trip, DiffPure-style denoising); CelebA-HQ portraits at 512×512.
  • Research Motivation: Non-consensual deepfakes of real people can now be made cheaply from a single public photo. A protection that individuals can apply themselves, offline and invisibly, raises the cost of such misuse. To be useful it must survive the simple cleanups an attacker applies first, and it must be judged on whether the person is still recognisable, not only on how much the pixels changed.
  • Research Objectives:
    1. Invisibility: the protected photo must look identical to the original: PSNR ≥ 38 dB and SSIM ≥ 0.95 on every one of the 100 test photos, with no pixel changed by more than 4/255.
    2. Protection: edits of a protected photo must stop matching the person. We measure this with the protection success rate (PSR): the share of edits that the ArcFace face-recognition model no longer matches to the original person. PSR must be clearly higher than for unprotected photos and for random noise of the same size, with paired 95% confidence intervals that exclude zero, and it is compared with published tools (PhotoGuard and Mist).
    3. Robustness and generality: measure protection after common cleanups (JPEG, resizing, diffusion-based denoising) and across several image editors, including some never used while making the protection, with a reproducible protocol.

2.2Literature Review & Research Gap

Adversarial protections for images target the parts of latent diffusion models [6]: the VAE encoder, which turns a photo into the compressed “latent” an editor starts from, or the UNet denoiser that performs the edit. Most published work protects artworks or fine-tuning data, uses budgets of 8–16/255 and reports image-quality changes; purification methods such as DiffPure [5] were designed to strip exactly this kind of perturbation.

Summary of Research Gap: There is little evidence on whether such perturbations remove a person’s identity from edits (measured with a face recogniser rather than pixel distances), whether they survive purification and everyday cleanups, and whether they do so while staying invisible on every photo at a small budget. DiffShield addresses this with a cleanup-aware, target-steered protection that also attacks the denoiser, and an identity-based evaluation with controls and paired statistics.

3Proposed Methodology & Solution Design

3.1End-to-End Methodological Workflow

The system has two halves that never share components. Half A (protection, offline) starts from a small random pattern and refines it for 40 steps. At each step it applies four randomly chosen cleanups to the current protected photo, scores the cleaned copies, and moves the pattern in the direction that raises the score, keeping every pixel within ±4/255. The result is the 8-bit PNG that the owner posts. Half B (attack and score) plays the attacker: it optionally cleans the posted photo with a cleanup Half A never used, edits it with one of five editors, compares the face in each edit with the original person using ArcFace [7], and reports the protection success rate with paired confidence intervals. Half B uses standard library implementations rather than the differentiable versions inside Half A, and adds cleanups and editors Half A never used (multi-step DiffPure-style cleanup, a pixel-space diffusion purifier, and four of the five editors), so robustness is not only measured on the training conditions.

3.2Mathematical Formulation & Algorithmic Design

  • Theoretical / Mathematical Formulation: let x be the photo, δ the noise, P a randomly drawn cleanup, and E the Stable Diffusion 1.5 VAE encoder, which turns an image into the latent an editor starts from. For each cleaned copy P(x+δ) the optimiser computes one score made of four parts, and averages it over 4 cleanups:
    1. Target pull (α = 0.188): the mean squared distance between the latent of the cleaned copy and an all-zeros latent, a fixed “useless” target. Smaller is better.
    2. Denoiser error (η = 0.012): how badly the editor’s UNet denoiser predicts the noise added to that latent at a random timestep between 50 and 300. Larger is better.
    3. Decoy (β = 0.546): the decoy distance, 1 − cosine similarity between the photo’s CLIP image embedding and the text “a potted plant”. Smaller is better.
    4. Visibility penalty (γ = 21.6): 1 − SSIM between the original and the published (uncleaned) protected photo. Smaller is better.
    Score = −α·(target distance) + η·(denoiser error) − β·(decoy distance) − γ·(1 − SSIM)
    The optimiser maximises this score subject to every pixel of δ staying within ±4/255.
  • Core Algorithmic Steps: (1) start δ with uniform random values within ±4/255; (2) for each of 40 steps, draw 4 cleanups from the training mixture: JPEG with random quality 50–95 (24.5% of draws), down-up resizing to 50–90% (24.5%), the SD VAE round trip (21%), and a one-step DiffPure-style purifier that adds diffusion noise at a random timestep 50–300 and removes it with one UNet step (30%); all four are differentiable; (3) compute the score on each cleaned copy and average the gradients; (4) move every pixel of δ by 1/255 in the direction that raises the score, clip to ±4/255 and round the protected photo to 8-bit; (5) after 40 steps, save the PNG with its PSNR and SSIM.
  • How the weights were chosen: by a procedure fixed in advance. 28 search and 12 confirmation photos were selected with MMD-critic [11], a method that picks a small set whose spread of CLIP embeddings matches the whole pool of 28,974 usable photos (test photos excluded); it was more representative than 1,000 random sets of the same size (p = 0.001). Optuna [12] then tried 25 weight settings on the search photos, scoring each by its average PSR over two editors and five cleanups and rejecting any setting that made even one photo visible. The best three were re-run at full quality on the confirmation photos, and the chosen setting was finally checked on the 100 test photos.
  • Dataset & Preprocessing Specifications: CelebA-HQ [16] portraits at 512×512 RGB, with CelebAMask-HQ [17] parsing masks for the inpainting editor. Fixed lists: 100 test photos (used only for the results in Section 5) and separate tuning photos as above. Preprocessing: centre crop and resize to 512 px PNG, a per-photo random seed derived from a hash, and image padding before face detection.
  • Evaluation Metrics:
    • PSR (protection success rate) = share of edits in which no face is detected or the ArcFace similarity to the original person is below the threshold τ. τ is the 95th percentile of the similarity between edits and photos of different people, so below τ the recogniser treats the edit as a stranger (τ = 0.1005 for Stable Diffusion 1.5). Higher is better.
    • Net protection = PSR with protection − PSR without protection under the same cleanup, because cleanups also change unprotected photos.
    • Invisibility: PSNR (pixel error, in dB) and SSIM (structural similarity) between the original and the protected photo, required on every photo.
    • Uncertainty: results are averaged per photo over seeds and prompts; 95% intervals come from a paired bootstrap that resamples photos (10,000 draws).
    • Audit: the share of edits with no detectable face and the mean similarity are reported next to PSR.

4Implementation Details

4.1Implementation Environment & Execution Setup

  • Hardware & Compute Specifications: Kaggle notebook sessions with 2× NVIDIA T4 GPUs (16 GB each); a local CPU machine for analysis, tables and tests. Measured cost: 274 GPU-seconds to protect one photo; per edit about 2.6 s (SD1.5), 18 s (SDXL at 1024 px) and 7 s (InstructPix2Pix and inpainting) on one T4.
  • Software & Libraries: Python 3, PyTorch, diffusers 0.40.0, transformers 5.16.1, accelerate 1.14.0, insightface 0.7.3 (ArcFace buffalo_l) with onnxruntime-gpu 1.22.0, facenet-pytorch (FaceNet [8] and MTCNN), Optuna 5.0.0, scikit-image 0.25.2, pandas 2.3.3, OpenCV.
  • Source Code Repository: https://github.com/Project-DiffShield/DiffShield. Every run is produced from a hash-stamped code bundle whose commit is printed in its log (final evaluation bundle 78ca757); one YAML configuration, logged seeds and library versions, and 377 CPU unit tests.

4.3Detailed Module Descriptions

  • Data Pipeline (scripts/prepare_data.py, data/splits/): reads CelebA-HQ, writes 512 px PNGs per split from frozen ID lists and records a manifest; the tuning-photo selector computes CLIP embeddings of the pool and picks the MMD-critic set, and one command reproduces the selection exactly.
  • Baselines (src/baselines/): every comparison method is registered as a method of the same pipeline, so it is protected, edited and scored exactly like DiffShield, on the same photos and seeds.
  • Protection Loop (src/protect/, scripts/run_protect.py): one configuration defines the weights and cleanup mixture; the loop runs the VAE in half precision with gradient checkpointing to fit a T4, averages gradients over the 4 cleanup draws, shards photos over both GPUs, checkpoints every few photos and writes the protected PNG, PSNR, SSIM and a loss trace.
  • End-to-End Integration: every Kaggle session is declared as a list of steps in kaggle/jobs.yaml and run by run_job.py (install, data, protect, evaluate, merge); protection and evaluation only exchange files, so each can be rerun alone; the evaluation writes one CSV row per (photo, seed, editor, prompt, strength, cleanup, method), and paired_compare.py produces every comparison in this report.

5.2Visual Results & Prototype Demonstration

Figure 2 summarises the main result. The prototype is a command-line demo (scripts/demo.py): it protects one photo, edits both the original and the protected copy with the same seed, and reports the protected photo’s PSNR and SSIM and the ArcFace similarity of each edit to the person.

Discussion of Results: Random noise of the same size gives 14.9% against 13.9% for unprotected photos, so DiffShield’s +37.3 points do not come from adding noise as such. The no-target ablation, which has the same cleanups and a denoiser term but only pushes the latent away, stays at 24.3%: adding the target pull is what lifts protection to 51.2% (+26.9 points [+23.0, +30.9]), because a fixed target gives the optimiser one consistent direction and drives the editor toward degraded output. The weight search confirmed the design rather than improving it: a hand-set version of the same design is statistically on par (+1.6 points [−0.9, +4.0]). Protection survives the SD-based DiffPure-style cleanup and the VAE round trip almost completely (net +35 to +42 points), is reduced but still positive under JPEG and resizing, and is removed by the pixel-space face purifier. PhotoGuard and Mist reach higher PSR without cleanup only by visibly altering every photo; under JPEG 75 DiffShield is ahead of PhotoGuard (+4.3 points [+0.7, +8.0]) and level with Mist, while under the VAE round trip and DiffPure-style cleanup the visible tools stay ahead. The audit shows the gain is not caused by faces disappearing (1.8% of edits have no face; counting only edits with a face, PSR is still 50.3%), and the mean similarity of edits to the person falls from 0.222 to 0.103. Protection is probabilistic per edit: only 4% of photos are protected in every seed and prompt, and the protected edits tend to look degraded or restyled rather than like a believable different person. Beyond SD1.5 image-to-image at moderate strength, protection is negligible: SDXL uses a different encoder, inpainting keeps the face region untouched, IP-Adapter FaceID takes identity from a face-recognition embedding, and at strength 0.3 the edit keeps almost everything while at 0.75 even unprotected edits lose the identity.

6Technical Justification, Design Trade-offs & Insights

  • Algorithmic & Theoretical Justification: image-to-image editors start from the VAE latent of the input photo, so controlling that latent controls the edit, and a fixed target gives the optimiser a consistent direction (the no-target ablation shows how much this matters). Optimising the average score over random cleanups (expectation over transformation [10], with sign-gradient steps as in projected gradient descent [9]) makes the noise survive the cleanups an attacker applies before editing; including a one-step DiffPure-style purifier in that mix targets diffusion-based cleanup directly, and the denoiser term additionally disturbs the editor’s UNet. The visibility penalty is placed on the published photo, the one people actually see. PSR operationalises “no longer the same person” with a face recogniser and an impostor threshold, which is a stricter and more relevant criterion than pixel distance.
  • Computational Complexity & Trade-offs: protecting one photo needs 40 × 4 = 160 forward and backward passes through the VAE encoder, plus a UNet pass whenever the one-step purifier or the denoiser term is used; memory stays within one T4 thanks to half precision and gradient checkpointing (measured 274 GPU-seconds per photo). In the weight search, large denoiser weights gave the worst protection of all settings (proxy PSR 7.5–12.1%) and the best settings kept it near 0.01, which is why its weight is small. On the 28 tuning photos, raising the budget from 4/255 to 6/255 raised PSR from 18.9% to 21.3% with 1 of 28 photos becoming visible, and 8/255 gave the same 21.3% with 12 of 28 visible; 4/255, fixed before any result, is therefore kept. In development tests on the tuning photos with the hand-set weights, running 100 optimisation steps instead of 40 changed PSR by only +0.5 points, so 40 steps are enough, and an extra identity loss on a FaceNet surrogate reduced PSR (−1.8 to −3.0 points) because it did not transfer to ArcFace and took budget from the main terms; it is therefore not part of the method.
  • Handling Edge Cases & Boundary Conditions: invisibility is enforced on every photo, not on averages, both in the weight search and in the final check; edits with no detectable face count as protected, so the no-face share is always reported next to PSR; face detection initially missed tight test crops, so images are padded before detection; a failing editor or missing model is isolated and logged instead of aborting a job; every job resumes from checkpoints; cleanups that change unprotected photos are handled by reporting net protection.

7Conclusion, Limitations & Future Scope

  • Conclusion: DiffShield protects face photos against Stable Diffusion 1.5 image-to-image editing while keeping every test photo invisible: 51.2% of edits no longer match the person, against 13.9% without protection and 24.0% for our first prototype, with paired intervals far from zero. Its protection survives SD-based DiffPure-style cleanup and the VAE round trip, and under JPEG it keeps more of its protection than PhotoGuard at far lower visibility. The target pull is the component that makes the difference; the weight search, the DiffPure-style purifier in training and the denoiser term were validated by a pre-registered procedure and keep the method on par with a hand-set version of the same design.
  • Current Limitations: no protection for SDXL, inpainting or IP-Adapter FaceID; JPEG and resizing remove most of the effect; a pixel-space diffusion purifier removes it completely; an adaptive attacker (a small network trained to undo the noise) removed our first prototype’s protection and was not run against the final method; protection is per edit, not per photo; one face recogniser scores the main results; PhotoGuard and Mist are our re-implementations at our budget; no human perception study; CelebA-HQ portraits only.
  • Future Scope: train against pixel-space diffusion purifiers and an adaptive attacker; an identity term on several face models including ArcFace, scored by an independent recogniser, to reach identity-conditioned editors; targets for the SDXL encoder; a human study of visibility and recognisability; everyday photos beyond CelebA-HQ.
