1Abstract

Diffusion-based image editors can turn any face photo posted online into a convincing fake of that person. DiffShield is a defensive tool that adds an almost invisible perturbation (L∞ ≤ 4/255) to a photo before it is posted, so that edits made from it no longer preserve the person’s identity. Existing protections such as PhotoGuard and Mist attack the Stable Diffusion encoder, but they are evaluated mainly with image-quality metrics, at larger budgets and rarely against purification. We redesigned our earlier prototype (v1) into two independent halves: a protection loop that optimises the perturbation with projected gradient descent over a random mixture of cleanups an attacker might apply, pulling the photo’s latent toward a fixed destructive target; and an evaluation harness that attacks the protected photo with 13 held-out cleanups and 5 editors and measures identity with an ArcFace face recogniser. All modules are implemented and were run on 100 CelebA-HQ test photos with 3 seeds. The target-steered method roughly doubles v1’s protection success rate (49.7% vs 24.0%; paired difference +25.7 points, 95% CI [+21.8, +29.8]) while every test photo stays invisible (PSNR ≥ 38 dB and SSIM ≥ 0.95), and it keeps more protection than PhotoGuard under JPEG compression. A re-tuned version that adds a DiffPure-style cleaner and a denoiser attack to training performs on par. Protection does not extend to SDXL, inpainting or IP-Adapter FaceID, and a pixel-space diffusion purifier removes it.

2Problem Definition, Research Motivation & Literature Review

2.1Problem Statement & Research Motivation

  • Problem Statement: Given a face photo x ∈ [0,1]512×512×3, find a perturbation δ with ‖δ‖∞ ≤ 4/255 such that (i) x+δ is visually identical to x, and (ii) edits of x+δ by a diffusion editor, possibly after a cleanup, are no longer recognised as the same person. Scope: a non-adaptive attacker using SD1.5 and SDXL image-to-image, InstructPix2Pix, SD inpainting or IP-Adapter FaceID, after any of 13 cleanups (JPEG, resizing, VAE round trip, DiffPure-style denoising, restoration); an adaptive attacker as a separate stress test; CelebA-HQ portraits.
  • Research Motivation: Non-consensual deepfakes of real people are now cheap to produce from a single public photo. A protection that individuals can apply themselves, offline and invisibly, shifts part of that cost back to the attacker. To be useful it must survive the simple cleanups an attacker applies first, and its effect must be measured on identity rather than on pixel change.
  • Research Objectives:
    1. Invisibility: PSNR ≥ 38 dB and SSIM ≥ 0.95 on every one of the 100 test photos at a 4/255 budget.
    2. Protection: a protection success rate (PSR) higher than clean photos, a random-noise control and v1, with paired 95% confidence intervals that exclude 0.
    3. Robustness and generality: measure protection under 13 held-out cleanups and 5 editors with a paired, reproducible protocol (fixed splits, seeds and logged runs).

2.2Literature Review & Research Gap

Adversarial protections for images target the components of latent diffusion models [6]: the VAE encoder that maps a photo to the latent the editor starts from, or the UNet denoiser. Most work protects artworks or fine-tuning data, uses budgets of 8–16/255 and reports image-quality changes; purification methods such as DiffPure [5] were designed to strip exactly this kind of perturbation.

Summary of Research Gap: There is little evidence on whether such perturbations remove a person’s identity from edits (measured with a face recogniser rather than pixel distances), whether they survive purification and ordinary cleanups, and whether they do so while staying invisible on every photo at a small budget. Our own v1 additionally had a mislabelled budget (“8/255” measured on [−1, 1] tensors is 4/255), no noise control and an evaluation on one editor, prompt and seed. DiffShield v2 addresses this gap with a cleanup-aware, target-steered protection and an identity-based, paired evaluation.

3Proposed Methodology & Solution Design

3.1End-to-End Methodological Workflow

The system has two halves that never share components. Half A (offline protection) takes a face photo, repeatedly samples a cleanup an attacker might use, scores the cleaned copy of the current protected photo with the loss below, takes a signed gradient step and projects back into the 4/255 budget; after 40 steps it outputs the 8-bit photo that would be posted. Half B (attack and score) applies one of 13 held-out cleanups and one of 5 editors to that photo, scores the identity of each edit against the original with ArcFace [7], and aggregates PSR with paired bootstrap intervals against clean photos, a noise control and v1. Because Half B only uses cleanups and editors Half A never saw, a gain cannot come from testing on the training conditions.

3.2Mathematical Formulation & Algorithmic Design

  • Theoretical / Mathematical Formulation: the protection solves
    max‖δ‖∞ ≤ ε   EP∼𝒫 [ α·Lvis(x, P(x+δ)) + η·Lden(P(x+δ)) − β·Lsem(P(x+δ)) − γ·Lstr(x, x+δ) − w·‖E(P(x+δ)) − zt‖22 ]
    where E is the SD1.5 VAE encoder, 𝒫 the training mixture of differentiable cleanups, Lvis the distance between the latents of the cleaned protected photo and of x (pushed up), Lden the UNet’s denoising error on the cleaned photo’s noised latent, Lsem = 1 − cos(CLIP(P(x+δ)), CLIP(“a potted plant”)), Lstr = 1 − SSIM(x, x+δ) on the published photo, and zt = 0 the destructive target latent. Constraint: ε = 4/255 on the 0–255 scale. Final weights: α = 0.55, β = 0.90, γ = 60.5, w = 1, η = 0 (re-tuned variant: α = 0.19, β = 0.55, γ = 21.6, η = 0.012 on timesteps 50–300).
  • Core Algorithmic Steps: (1) initialise δ = 0; (2) for each of T = 40 iterations, draw K = 4 cleanups from 𝒫 (JPEG with random quality, down-up resizing with a random scale, VAE round trip, and in the re-tuned variant a one-step DiffPure-style SD purifier in 30% of draws); (3) average the gradient of the objective over the draws; (4) update δ ← clip[−ε, ε](δ + a·sign(∇δ)) with a = 1/255 and round x+δ to 8-bit; (5) save the protected PNG and its PSNR/SSIM. Evaluation then edits every protected photo with each editor, prompt, strength and seed, after each held-out cleanup.
  • Dataset & Preprocessing Specifications: CelebA-HQ [16] portraits at 512×512 RGB, with CelebAMask-HQ [17] parsing masks for the inpainting editor. Fixed lists: test 100 (including all 70 of v1’s photos, so v1 is compared on the same photos), validation 20, train 20, messy 20 (pose, blur, lighting, occlusion), adaptive-attacker pool 120; the final re-tuning used 28 search and 12 confirmation photos chosen by MMD-critic [11] from a pool of 28,974, never test photos. Preprocessing: centre crop and resize to 512 px PNG, per-image seeds derived from a hash, and image padding before face detection.
  • Evaluation Metrics: PSR = (1/N) Σi 1[ no face in ei ∨ s(ei, x) < τ ], where ei are the edits, s the ArcFace cosine similarity and τ the 95th percentile of similarity between edits and sources of different people on clean photos (τ = 0.1005 for SD1.5). Net protection = PSR(method) − PSR(clean) under the same cleanup, because cleanups also change clean photos. Invisibility: PSNR = 10·log10(1/MSE) and SSIM between x and x+δ, required on every photo. Uncertainty: per-photo means over seeds and prompts, then a paired bootstrap over photos (95% intervals). The no-face rate and mean similarity are always reported next to PSR.

4.3Detailed Module Descriptions

  • Data Pipeline (scripts/prepare_data.py, data/splits/): reads CelebA-HQ, writes 512 px PNGs per split from frozen ID lists, and records a manifest; the tuning-photo selector computes CLIP embeddings of the pool and picks MMD-critic prototypes and criticisms, re-checked against 1,000 random subsets (search set more representative than chance, p = 0.001).
  • Baselines (src/baselines/): v1 (40 PGD steps with random JPEG/crop, weights from its own Optuna run), noise controls at the same budget, PhotoGuard and Mist at our 4/255 budget, and an adaptive autoencoder attacker trained on 120 other photos; all are registered as methods so the evaluation treats them exactly like DiffShield.
  • Protection Loop (src/protect/, scripts/run_protect.py): method presets resolve to one configuration; the loop runs the VAE in fp16 autocast with gradient checkpointing, averages gradients over K cleanup draws, shards photos over both GPUs, checkpoints every few photos and writes the protected PNG, PSNR/SSIM/LPIPS and the loss trace.
  • End-to-End Integration: every Kaggle session is declared as a list of steps in kaggle/jobs.yaml and run by run_job.py (install, data, protect, evaluate, merge); protection and evaluation only exchange files, so each can be rerun alone; the evaluation writes one CSV row per (photo, seed, editor, prompt, strength, cleanup, method), and paired_compare.py and make_tables.py produce every number in this report.

5.2Visual Results & Prototype Demonstration

Discussion of Results: Random noise at the same budget gives 14.9% against 13.9% for clean photos (+1.0 points [−1.0, +3.1]), so the effect is not caused by noise as such; v1 gains +10.1 points [+7.2, +13.0] over clean photos. Training against cleanups without a target term (the ablation) does not beat v1, because pushing the latent “away” from the clean one has no direction. Pulling it toward a fixed destructive target gives the proposed method a paired gain over v1 of +25.7 points [+21.8, +29.8] with no visible photo; its edits are degraded or restyled rather than turned into a believable different person. Under JPEG 75 it is ahead of PhotoGuard (+5.0 points [+1.3, +9.0]) at far lower visibility, while PhotoGuard and Mist protect more without cleanup only by visibly altering every photo. The re-tuned variant with a DiffPure-style cleaner and a denoiser term is on par (+1.6 points [−0.9, +4.0]), so the hand-set weights were retained. Protection is limited to image-to-image editing with SD1.5: SDXL, inpainting (the face region is kept, so its pixels never reach the model) and IP-Adapter FaceID (identity comes from a face-recognition embedding) are not protected.

6Technical Justification, Design Trade-offs & Insights

  • Algorithmic & Theoretical Justification: image-to-image editors start from the VAE latent of the input photo, so controlling that latent controls the edit; a fixed target gives the optimiser a consistent direction, which is why the target term doubles protection where an undirected latent shift does not. Optimising the expectation over a cleanup mixture (expectation over transformation [10], with PGD [9]) makes the perturbation survive the transformations an attacker applies before editing. The visibility penalty is placed on the published photo, the one people actually see. PSR operationalises “no longer the same person” with a face recogniser and an impostor threshold, which is a stricter and more relevant target than pixel distance.
  • Computational Complexity & Trade-offs: protection costs T·K forward and backward passes through the VAE encoder (plus the UNet when the one-step cleaner or denoiser term is on), i.e. 160 passes for T = 40, K = 4; memory is bounded by one 512 px graph thanks to gradient checkpointing and fp16. Measured: about 2 GPU-minutes per photo for the proposed method and 274 GPU-seconds for the re-tuned one. More iterations do not help (100 steps: +0.5 points on the tuning photos), so 40 was kept. Budget is the main lever but trades directly against invisibility: on the tuning photos 6/255 adds 2.7 points with 1 of 28 photos visible, 8/255 gives the same gain with 12 of 28 visible, so 4/255 (fixed before results) remains the headline. Among the target weights, “high” adds 3.1 points but makes 4 of 100 photos visible, so “mid” is used.
  • Handling Edge Cases & Boundary Conditions: invisibility is checked on every photo, not on averages (the earlier Optuna-tuned method passed on average but failed on 29 of 100 photos); edits with no detectable face count as protected, so the no-face rate (1.4–2.6% for the proposed method) is always reported; face detection initially missed 38 of 100 tight test crops and now pads the image first; a failing editor or missing model is isolated and logged instead of aborting a job; every job resumes from checkpoints; cleanups that change clean photos are handled by reporting net protection. Known failure cases: JPEG and resizing cut the effect by more than half, a pixel-space DDPM purifier removes it completely, and a small adaptive autoencoder attacker brings v1 back to the clean level.

7Conclusion, Limitations & Future Scope

  • Conclusion: We corrected v1’s budget (4/255, not 8/255), built a fair, identity-based and fully reproducible evaluation, and showed that a target-steered, cleanup-aware perturbation roughly doubles v1’s protection of face photos against SD1.5 image-to-image editing while keeping every test photo invisible, and keeps more of its protection than visible tools under JPEG and resizing. A systematic re-tuning with a DiffPure-style cleaner and a denoiser attack confirmed the method rather than improving it.
  • Current Limitations: no protection for SDXL, inpainting or IP-Adapter FaceID; a pixel-space diffusion purifier and an adaptive attacker remove the protection (the adaptive attacker was not run on the final method); an identity loss on one surrogate face model (FaceNet) did not transfer to ArcFace; one face recogniser scores the main results (second-recogniser check: [insert F1 result]); PhotoGuard and Mist are our re-implementations at our budget; no human perception study; CelebA-HQ portraits only.
  • Future Scope: run the adaptive attacker against the final method and train against pixel-space diffusion purifiers; an identity term on an ensemble of face models including ArcFace, scored by an independent recogniser, for identity-conditioned editors; SDXL-aware targets; a human study of visibility and recognisability; extending beyond CelebA-HQ to everyday photos.
